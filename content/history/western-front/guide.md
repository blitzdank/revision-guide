## The exam

Paper 1 Section A (16 marks), studied alongside Medicine in Britain. This is a **historic environment** topic — you're given sources in the exam and asked about a specific site/environment, so you need both factual knowledge *and* practice interpreting sources.

Typical questions:
- **Q1(a)/(b)** — describe features (straightforward recall, AO1).
- **Q2(a)** — assess how useful two contemporary sources are for a specific enquiry, using your own contextual knowledge.
- **Q2(b)** — suggest a follow-up question/enquiry.

> **Exam tip:** "How useful" questions want you to judge the sources using **both** their content **and** your own knowledge of the period — a source can be useful even if it's biased, as long as you explain *what* it's useful for and *why*.

## The trench system

Fighting on the Western Front centred on major battles at the **Ypres salient**, **the Somme**, **Arras** and **Cambrai**. Trenches were organised into frontline and support lines, with communication trenches connecting them; the terrain (mud, waterlogged ground in Flanders) badly affected movement, transport and medical evacuation.

## Wounds and injuries

- Rifle and explosive (shell/shrapnel) wounds were the most common injury type.
- **Shrapnel** caused especially severe, dirty wounds.
- **Wound infection** (including gas gangrene, from soil bacteria entering wounds) was a huge killer — often more dangerous than the initial injury.
- **Head injuries** became more common and better understood as trench warfare continued; the **Brodie helmet** was introduced partly in response.
- **Gas attacks** (chlorine, phosgene, mustard gas) created a new category of injury medicine had to adapt to quickly.

## The chain of treatment

Getting a wounded soldier from the frontline to proper treatment involved several stages — know this chain in order, it's a common focus:

1. **Stretcher bearers** collected the wounded under fire.
2. **Regimental Aid Post** — first aid, close to the front line, run by the RAMC (Royal Army Medical Corps) and nurses.
3. **Field Ambulance** — a mobile unit, not a vehicle, that gave further treatment.
4. **Dressing Station** — more thorough treatment and triage (deciding who needed urgent care).
5. **Casualty Clearing Station** — the first place proper surgery could happen, close enough to the front to treat urgent cases quickly.
6. **Base Hospital** — full hospital treatment, further from the front; from here, serious cases might be sent back to Britain.

Transport between these stages used **horse-drawn and motor ambulances**.

## Innovations driven by the war

- New **surgery and infection-control techniques**, driven by the sheer scale of wound infection.
- The **Thomas splint** dramatically reduced deaths from broken femurs by stabilising the leg during transport.
- **Mobile X-ray units** (including vehicles fitted out for the purpose) let doctors locate shrapnel/bullets without surgery first.
- The first **blood bank**, used at the Battle of Cambrai (1917), allowed stored blood to be transfused rather than relying only on direct donor-to-patient transfusion.
- The **underground hospital at Arras**, built into tunnels, is a specific named site worth knowing for source questions about treatment environments.

> **Exam tip:** For the "describe two features" question, pick features you can say something specific and accurate about (e.g. "casualty clearing stations were close enough to the front for urgent surgery, but far enough back to be safer than a dressing station") rather than vague ones.
