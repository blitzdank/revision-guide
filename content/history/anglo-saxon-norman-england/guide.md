## The exam

Paper 2, Booklet B (British depth study, 32 marks). Questions:
- **Q1(a)/(b)** — describe features (compulsory, straightforward recall).
- **Q2** — explain the causes of something (compulsory).
- **Q3 or Q4** — a choice of judgement question (similarity/difference, change/continuity, causation, consequence, or significance).

> **Exam tip:** This is a **depth study** — short, intense period (1060–88), so you're expected to know specific detail (names, dates, events), not just broad trends.

## Key Topic 1 (1060–66): the succession crisis and conquest

- Late Anglo-Saxon England: government, economy and towns were relatively sophisticated for the time; the Church had major influence.
- The **Godwin family** held huge power — **Harold Godwinson** was the most powerful noble in England.
- Harold's **embassy to Normandy** (likely 1064) is disputed — Norman sources claim he swore an oath to support William's claim to the throne.
- **Rival claimants** to the throne on Edward the Confessor's death (January 1066): **William of Normandy**, **Harald Hardrada** of Norway, and **Edgar Aethling** (the young Anglo-Saxon claimant). The **Witan** (the council of nobles) chose **Harold Godwinson**, who was crowned immediately.
- **Battle of Gate Fulford** and **Battle of Stamford Bridge** (September 1066) — Harold defeated Harald Hardrada's invasion in the north, then had to force-march his army south.
- **Battle of Hastings** (14 October 1066) — reasons for William's victory: Harold's exhausted, depleted army; tactical factors (the feigned retreats, Norman cavalry and archers vs. the Anglo-Saxon shield wall); and Harold's death partway through the battle.

## Key Topic 2 (1066–87): establishing control

- William's early approach: the **submission of the earls** (late 1066) tried to secure loyalty without full conquest.
- He **rewarded his Norman followers** with land, including powerful **Marcher earldoms** on the Welsh border to defend and control the frontier.
- **Castles** — reasons for building them (military control, symbol of power, safe bases) and typical features (motte and bailey) spread rapidly across England.
- **Anglo-Saxon resistance**: the revolt of **Edwin and Morcar (1068)**; the **Edgar Aethling/Northern rebellions (1069)**; **Hereward the Wake's** resistance at **Ely (1070–71)**.
- The **Harrying of the North (1069–70)** — William's brutal campaign of destruction across northern England in response to rebellion, causing famine and depopulation; a key event for questions on the *impact* of Norman control.
- **Landownership changed dramatically** — Anglo-Saxon nobles were largely replaced by Norman lords.
- The **Revolt of the Earls (1075)** — a late, failed rebellion by Norman as well as English nobles, showing resistance wasn't purely an Anglo-Saxon vs. Norman issue.

## Key Topic 3: government, Church and society under the Normans

- **Feudal hierarchy**: the King at the top, granting land to **tenants-in-chief** in exchange for **knight service**, who in turn granted land further down the hierarchy.
- **The Church**: **Archbishop Stigand** (Anglo-Saxon) was replaced by **Lanfranc** (Norman) — part of a wider replacement of English Church leadership.
- **Norman government**: the continuing importance of the **sheriff** in local administration; the creation of royal "**forest**" land (hunting reserves with their own harsh laws).
- The **Domesday Book/survey** (1086) — a huge, detailed land and resource survey of England, used for taxation and establishing who held what land; a landmark piece of evidence for historians.
- **Norman aristocracy and culture** — the use of the French language among the new ruling class; figures like **Bishop Odo** (William's half-brother) show how Church and state power overlapped.
- William I's character and final years: the **revolt of his son Robert (1077–80)**; his death (1087) and the **disputed succession** between **William Rufus** and **Robert**, with Odo backing Robert — showing that Norman control, even under William himself, was never fully secure.

> **Exam tip:** For "explain the causes" questions, aim for at least two to three distinct, well-explained causes rather than one cause described at length — examiners want breadth *and* depth.
