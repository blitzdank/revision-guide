## The exam

Paper 1 Section B (36 marks), alongside the Western Front historic environment in Section A. This is a **thematic study** spanning c1250 to today — the questions test how and why ideas about **causes**, **prevention** and **treatment** of disease changed (or didn't) across four periods.

Typical questions:
- **Q3** — explain one way two things were similar/different (spans a century or more).
- **Q4** — explain why change happened at a particular pace (rapid/slow/continued) across a long period.
- **Q5 or Q6** — a 16-mark judgement essay with a given statement, e.g. "How far do you agree that [X] was the main reason for change in medicine in this period?" (up to 4 of these marks are for spelling/punctuation/grammar).

> **Exam tip:** These questions almost always span more than one of the four periods below. Revise the periods individually, but also practise tracking **one theme** (e.g. government involvement, or the role of science) all the way through c1250–present.

## c1250–1500: medieval medicine

- **Ideas about causes:** supernatural/religious explanations (God's punishment); the **Four Humours** theory (inherited from the ancient Greeks) — illness caused by an imbalance of blood, phlegm, black bile, yellow bile; **miasma** (bad air); **Galen**'s theories still dominated medical teaching, even where wrong.
- **Prevention and treatment:** religious actions (prayer, pilgrimage); bloodletting and purging to rebalance the humours; trying to purify the air. Treatment came from physicians, apothecaries and barber-surgeons; hospitals mostly gave rest and care rather than medical treatment; most care still happened at home, in the community.
- **Case study: the Black Death (1348–49).** Explanations at the time (miasma, God's punishment, astrology); contemporary attempts at prevention/treatment reflected those beliefs rather than any real understanding of disease.

## c1500–1700: the Renaissance

- Change was slow despite new ideas, because Galen's authority was still hard to challenge.
- **Thomas Sydenham** pushed a more scientific, observation-based approach to diagnosis rather than relying on old theory.
- The **printing press** and the **Royal Society** (founded 1660) helped spread new ideas faster and gave scientists a way to share and test each other's work.
- **Vesalius** corrected errors in Galen's anatomy through dissection, influencing how doctors were trained.
- **Case study: William Harvey** — discovered the circulation of blood, disproving part of Galen's theory, though it took decades to change treatment in practice.
- **Case study: the Great Plague of London (1665)** — explanations and responses were still largely medieval (miasma, quarantine, prayer), showing how little practical change had happened yet.

## c1700–1900: the Industrial period

This is where the pace of change speeds up dramatically — a strong period for "explain why change was rapid" questions.

- **Germ Theory** (**Pasteur**, 1861) finally gave a *correct* explanation for disease, replacing centuries of wrong theory — one of the most significant turning points in the whole topic. **Koch** built on this, identifying the specific microbes behind individual diseases.
- **Florence Nightingale** transformed nursing standards and hospital hygiene.
- Anaesthetics and antiseptics made surgery survivable, not just possible.
- The **Public Health Act (1875)** made local government responsible for sanitation — a major shift toward government-led prevention (compare to medieval reliance on religion/the individual).
- **Case study: Edward Jenner and vaccination** — developed the smallpox vaccination (1796) using evidence/observation, decades before Germ Theory explained *why* it worked.
- **Case study: Cholera in London, 1854** — **John Snow** traced an outbreak to the Broad Street water pump, showing disease spread through contaminated water — a landmark use of evidence-based investigation, and a turning point for public health action.

## c1900–present: modern medicine

- Explanations now include **genetic and lifestyle factors**, not just infection.
- Diagnosis uses blood tests, scans and monitors — technology transforms what's possible.
- The **NHS (1948)** made healthcare free at the point of use — the biggest government intervention in the whole topic.
- "**Magic bullets**" and **antibiotics** transformed treatment of infection.
- High-tech hospital treatment, mass vaccination programmes and lifestyle campaigns (smoking, diet) show prevention becoming a government and public-health project, not just personal/religious.
- **Case study: Fleming, Florey and Chain and penicillin** — discovery (Fleming, 1928) vs. the much later development into a usable mass treatment (Florey and Chain, WWII) — a good example of the gap between a discovery and a working treatment.
- **Case study: the fight against lung cancer today** — shows the ongoing, unfinished nature of medical progress.

> **Exam tip:** For "how far do you agree" essays, decide early whether you're arguing *for* the statement, *against* it, or a balanced view — then structure each paragraph around a different factor (science, religion, government, technology, war, communication), always linking back to the given statement.
