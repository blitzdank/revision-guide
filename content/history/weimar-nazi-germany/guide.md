## The exam

Paper 3 (52 marks) — a **modern depth study**, with a distinctive second half focused on historical **interpretations** (two different historians'/sources' views on the same event), which doesn't appear elsewhere in the course.

- **Section A (16 marks):** Q1 — infer information from one source; then a choice of causation question.
- **Section B (36 marks, all compulsory):** Q3(a) — assess how useful two sources are for an enquiry; Q3(b) — explain **how** two given interpretations differ; Q3(c) — explain **why** they differ; Q3(d) — evaluate one interpretation using the other interpretation plus your own knowledge (up to 4 marks here are SPaG).

> **Exam tip:** Q3(b) and Q3(c) are often confused. (b) wants you to identify *what* the two interpretations actually disagree about — be specific, quote briefly from each. (c) wants you to explain *why* they disagree — different sources used, different focus/emphasis, different historians' perspectives or purposes.

## Key Topic 1 (1918–29): the Weimar Republic's origins and recovery

- End of WWI: the **Kaiser's abdication**, the **armistice** (November 1918), and the creation of a new democratic republic.
- The **Weimar Constitution** — strengths (proportional representation, a Bill of Rights) and weaknesses (proportional representation also caused unstable coalition governments; Article 48 let the President rule by emergency decree).
- The "**stab in the back**" myth — the false claim that Germany's army hadn't really been defeated, but betrayed by politicians at home; fed resentment toward the new republic.
- The **Treaty of Versailles** — harsh terms (territory loss, military restrictions, reparations, the "war guilt" clause) widely resented across Germany.
- Early threats: the **Spartacist uprising** (left-wing) and the **Kapp Putsch** (right-wing, 1920) — the new republic faced extremism from both sides.
- **1923 crisis**: **hyperinflation** (savings became worthless) and the **French occupation of the Ruhr** (after Germany defaulted on reparations) nearly destroyed the republic.
- **Stresemann's recovery**: the **Rentenmark** (new stable currency), the **Dawes Plan** and **Young Plan** (restructured reparations with American loans), the **Locarno Pact** and **League of Nations** membership (improved international standing).
- Changes to living standards and women's position, and cultural changes (architecture — Bauhaus, art, cinema) show the "Golden Age" alongside continuing underlying tensions.

## Key Topic 2 (1919–33): the rise of Hitler

- Hitler joined the **German Workers' Party**, which became the **Nazi Party** (1919–20), built around the **Twenty-Five Point Programme** and the paramilitary **SA**.
- The **Munich Putsch** (1923) — a failed attempt to seize power; Hitler's trial and imprisonment led to **Mein Kampf** and a shift toward gaining power through legal, electoral means.
- Nazi support was limited 1924–28 (economic recovery left little appetite for extremism) — the **Bamberg Conference (1926)** unified the party's direction under Hitler.
- The **Wall Street Crash/Depression** caused mass unemployment, which boosted support for both the Nazis and the Communists.
- Nazi appeal 1929–32: effective **propaganda**, the **SA**'s visible strength, and promises that cut across class lines.
- The **1932 elections** made the Nazis the largest party (though not a majority); **Hitler became Chancellor in January 1933** through backroom dealing — **Hindenburg** (President) and **von Papen** believed they could control Hitler by bringing him into government.

## Key Topic 3 (1933–39): consolidating the dictatorship

- The **Reichstag Fire** (February 1933) was used to justify emergency powers; the **Enabling Act** (March 1933) let Hitler pass laws without the Reichstag, effectively ending democracy; other political parties and trade unions were banned.
- The **SA** under **Röhm** became a threat even to Hitler (demanding a "second revolution"); the **Night of the Long Knives** (1934) eliminated Röhm and other rivals.
- **Hindenburg's death** (August 1934) let Hitler merge the roles of Chancellor and President into **Führer**; the army swore a personal **oath of loyalty** to him.
- Control mechanisms: the **Gestapo** (secret police), the **SS**, and **concentration camps** for political opponents.
- **Goebbels** and the **Ministry of Propaganda** controlled censorship, mass rallies, and used events like the **1936 Berlin Olympics** for image-building; control extended into culture and the arts.
- The **Concordat** with the Catholic Church and the "**Reich Church**" tried to manage religious institutions; opposition still existed — **Niemöller** (Church), and youth groups like the **Swing Youth** and **Edelweiss Pirates**.

## Key Topic 4 (1933–39): life in Nazi Germany

- **Women**: Nazi ideology pushed marriage, family and childbearing ("Kinder, Küche, Kirche" — children, kitchen, church) over employment and changed expectations around appearance.
- **Youth**: the **Hitler Youth** and **League of German Maidens** indoctrinated young people; education was tightly controlled to reinforce Nazi ideology.
- **Employment**: unemployment fell sharply through labour schemes, the **autobahns**, and **rearmament**; the **Labour Front** replaced trade unions; **Strength Through Joy** and **Beauty of Labour** schemes aimed to boost worker morale and productivity.
- **Persecution**: of Slavs, Roma and Sinti, homosexuals, and disabled people (forced sterilisation, later worse); and of **Jews** specifically — the **1933 boycott** of Jewish businesses, the **Nuremberg Laws (1935)** stripping citizenship rights, and **Kristallnacht (1938)**, a coordinated, violent pogrom marking a major escalation.

> **Exam tip:** For Q3(d) ("evaluate using the other interpretation and your own knowledge"), you genuinely need to bring in extra facts beyond what's in either source — this question specifically rewards contextual knowledge, not just source analysis.
