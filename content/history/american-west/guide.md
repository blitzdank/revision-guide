## The exam

Paper 2, Booklet P (period study, 32 marks). Questions:
- **Q1(a)/(b)** — explain a consequence of something.
- **Q2** — an **analytical narrative**: write an account explaining why events unfolded as they did (structure this as a flowing explanation, not a list).
- **Q3** — choose **2 of 3** parts, each on the significance of a named event, person or development.

> **Exam tip:** Q2's "analytical narrative" wants connected reasoning — "X happened, which led to Y, which caused Z" — not a list of facts in order. Use linking phrases like "as a result," "this meant that," "consequently."

## Key Topic 1 (1835–62): early settlement and tension

- **Plains Indigenous peoples**: social/tribal structure, nomadic life following buffalo, spiritual beliefs about land (not owned, shared/sacred), warfare customs, and views on property very different from white settlers'.
- **Migration west**: the **Oregon Trail** (from 1836), driven by **Manifest Destiny** (the belief Americans were destined to expand west) and the **California Gold Rush (1849)**. The **Donner Party** disaster (1846–47) and **Mormon migration** (also 1846–47) show the real dangers of the journey.
- **Growing tension**: the US government created a "**Permanent Indian Frontier**" to separate settlers and Indigenous peoples — but this broke down almost immediately under migration pressure. The first **Fort Laramie Treaty (1851)** tried to define tribal territories; the **Indian Appropriations Act (1851)** began forcing tribes onto reservations.
- Early settlements saw **lawlessness** before formal government structures caught up.

## Key Topic 2 (1862–76): development of the Plains

- **Transport**: the **Pacific Railroad Act (1862)** and the completion of the **First Transcontinental Railroad (1869)** transformed how fast people, goods and soldiers could move west.
- **Farming**: the **Homestead Act (1862)** gave settlers land if they farmed it for five years; the **Timber Culture Act (1873)** encouraged tree-planting on the largely treeless plains.
- **Cattle industry**: key figures include **Iliff**, **McCoy** and **Goodnight**; towns like **Abilene** became major cattle-trail destinations; the cowboy's role changed as the industry became more organised.
- **Impact on Indigenous peoples**: railroads, cattle and gold mining all encroached on tribal land and disrupted buffalo herds. The second **Fort Laramie Treaty (1868)** again tried (and again largely failed) to manage this.
- **Conflict**: **Little Crow's War (1862)**, the **Sand Creek Massacre (1864)** — US troops killed a peaceful Cheyenne/Arapaho camp — and **Red Cloud's War (1866–68)**, one of the few conflicts where Indigenous forces won significant concessions.

## Key Topic 3 (1876–95): conflict and conquest

- Farming and ranching changed further; the brutal **winter of 1886–87** devastated herds and effectively ended the "open range" cattle era.
- The **Exoduster movement** (Black settlers moving to Kansas, 1879) and the **Oklahoma Land Rush (1893)** show the frontier still expanding even as its character changed; the "Indian Frontier" was formally closed.
- **Law and order**: names like **Billy the Kid**, **Wyatt Earp** and the **shootout at the OK Corral (1881)**; the **Johnson County War (1892)** — a conflict between large and small ranchers.
- **The Battle of the Little Bighorn (1876)** — a major Indigenous victory over Custer's forces, but one that provoked a harsher US military response.
- **Wounded Knee Massacre (1890)** — the brutal, effective end of major armed Indigenous resistance on the Plains.
- The **extermination of the buffalo**, confinement to **reservations**, and the **Dawes Act (1887)** (which broke up reservation land into individual plots, undermining traditional tribal landholding) together destroyed the Plains Indigenous way of life described in Key Topic 1.

> **Exam tip:** For the significance questions (Q3), "significance" means explaining the *impact and consequences* of something — not just describing what it was. Always ask: what changed because of this?
