## Pathogens

A **pathogen** is a microorganism that causes disease. The four types:

- **Bacteria** — e.g. **Salmonella** (food poisoning) and **gonorrhoea** (a sexually transmitted infection). Bacteria can produce toxins that damage cells.
- **Viruses** — e.g. **measles** (spread by droplets in the air) and **HIV** (initially attacks the immune system; can lead to AIDS if untreated), and **tobacco mosaic virus** (a plant pathogen).
- **Fungi** — e.g. **rose black spot** (a plant disease, spread by water/wind).
- **Protists** — e.g. **malaria**, spread via mosquitoes acting as a vector (something that carries a pathogen without being infected by it itself).

## The body's defences

The body has physical/chemical barriers **before** the immune system even gets involved:
- **Skin** — a physical barrier, and produces antimicrobial secretions.
- **Nose** — hairs and mucus trap particles including pathogens.
- **Trachea and bronchi** — lined with mucus (traps pathogens) and cilia (tiny hairs that waft the mucus up and away from the lungs).
- **Stomach** — hydrochloric acid kills most pathogens that are swallowed.

If a pathogen gets past these barriers, **white blood cells** respond in three main ways:
1. **Phagocytosis** — engulfing and digesting the pathogen.
2. **Antibody production** — producing antibodies specific to the antigens on that pathogen's surface, which help destroy it.
3. **Antitoxin production** — neutralising toxins produced by the pathogen.

## Vaccination

A vaccine contains a small, safe amount of a dead or inactive form of a pathogen (or parts of it). It triggers the immune system to produce antibodies **without** causing the disease. If the real pathogen is encountered later, the body can respond much faster — producing antibodies quickly enough to prevent illness, because memory cells from the first exposure recognise it immediately.

> **Exam tip:** "Explain how vaccination protects a person" questions want the **mechanism** (antibody production triggered, memory cells created, faster response next time) — not just "it stops you getting ill."

## Treating disease

- **Antibiotics** (e.g. **penicillin**) kill bacteria — but **do not work on viruses**, since viruses reproduce inside human cells in a completely different way bacteria don't.
- **Painkillers** treat the *symptoms* of a disease, but don't kill the pathogen causing it.
- Antibiotic **overuse** contributes to antibiotic-resistant strains of bacteria developing — a major modern public health concern.

## Discovering and developing drugs

Many drugs originally came from natural sources:
- **Digitalis** (a heart drug) — originally from **foxgloves**.
- **Aspirin** (a painkiller) — originally from **willow trees**.
- **Penicillin** — discovered by Alexander Fleming from the mould *Penicillium*.

Modern drugs are typically developed synthetically but still tested through preclinical (lab/animal) and clinical (human) trials for safety and effectiveness before being approved.

> **Exam tip:** Keep your named examples (pathogen type → named disease; drug → natural source) genuinely memorised — these specific names come up often as easy marks if you know them, and are easy marks lost if you don't.
