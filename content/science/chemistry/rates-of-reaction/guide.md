## Collision theory

For a reaction to happen, particles must **collide** with enough energy — the minimum energy needed is called the **activation energy**. Anything that increases the **frequency** of collisions, or the **energy** of collisions, speeds up the reaction.

## The five factors affecting rate

1. **Concentration** (of a solution) — more particles in the same volume means more frequent collisions.
2. **Pressure** (of a gas) — more particles in the same volume means more frequent collisions (the gas equivalent of concentration).
3. **Surface area** (of a solid) — a bigger surface area exposes more particles to collide with, without changing the total amount of substance (e.g. crushing a solid into powder).
4. **Temperature** — particles move faster, so collisions are both **more frequent** and have **more energy** — this is why temperature has such a big effect on rate.
5. **Catalysts** — provide an alternative reaction pathway with a **lower activation energy**, without being used up themselves.

## Measuring and calculating rate

$$\text{mean rate of reaction} = \frac{\text{quantity of reactant used (or product formed)}}{\text{time}}$$

Units are usually g/s or cm³/s (or mol/s at Higher tier).

**Example:** 2.4 g of magnesium reacts completely with acid in 40 seconds. Find the mean rate.

$$\text{rate} = \frac{2.4 \text{ g}}{40 \text{ s}} = 0.06 \text{ g/s}$$

### Finding rate from a graph (Higher tier)

On a graph of product formed (or reactant used) against time:
- The **mean rate** over the whole reaction is the gradient from start to end.
- The rate **at a specific point in time** (the reaction isn't usually a straight line — it slows as reactants are used up) is found by drawing a **tangent** to the curve at that point, then calculating the gradient of the tangent:

$$\text{gradient} = \frac{\text{change in y}}{\text{change in x}}$$

> **Exam tip:** A steeper graph/tangent means a faster rate at that moment. The curve is always steepest at the very start (highest concentration of reactants) and flattens out as the reaction nears completion.

## Required practical: effect of concentration on rate

Investigate how changing the **concentration** of a reactant affects the rate of reaction — typically either:
- Measuring the **volume of gas produced** over time (e.g. using a gas syringe), or
- Timing how long it takes for a **colour change or precipitate** to obscure a mark (e.g. the "disappearing cross" experiment with sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid, which produces a cloudy sulfur precipitate).

**Variables:**
- **Independent variable**: concentration of the reactant being tested.
- **Dependent variable**: rate of reaction (volume of gas per time, or time taken for the colour change).
- **Control variables**: temperature, volume of solution, and (if relevant) surface area of any solid — keeping these constant is what makes the comparison fair.

> **Exam tip:** "Explain why the results are a fair test" wants you to name the specific variables that were controlled and why changing them would also have affected the rate — not just "it was a fair test because we controlled the variables."
