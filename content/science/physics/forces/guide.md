## Speed, distance and time

$$s = vt$$

(distance = speed × time) — rearrange as needed: $v = \dfrac{s}{t}$.

**Example:** a car travels at 15 m/s for 40 s. Find the distance travelled.

$$s = 15 \times 40 = 600 \text{ m}$$

## Acceleration

$$a = \frac{\Delta v}{t}$$

(acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken) — a **negative** acceleration means the object is slowing down (decelerating).

**Example:** a cyclist speeds up from 2 m/s to 12 m/s in 5 s. Find the acceleration.

$$a = \frac{12-2}{5} = \frac{10}{5} = 2 \text{ m/s}^2$$

## Newton's laws and force

**Newton's first law**: an object stays at rest or moving at a constant velocity **unless** a resultant force acts on it.

**Newton's second law**:

$$F = ma$$

(force = mass × acceleration). **Given in the exam.**

**Example:** a resultant force of 600 N acts on a 150 kg object. Find its acceleration.

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{600}{150} = 4 \text{ m/s}^2$$

**Newton's third law**: every action has an equal and opposite reaction — when object A exerts a force on object B, B exerts an equal, opposite force back on A.

## Weight

$$W = mg$$

(weight = mass × gravitational field strength). On Earth, $g \approx 9.8 \text{ N/kg}$ (sometimes rounded to 10 N/kg). **Weight is a force (measured in newtons) and is not the same as mass** (measured in kg) — weight changes depending on gravitational field strength (e.g. less on the Moon), mass doesn't.

**Example:** find the weight of a 60 kg person on Earth ($g = 9.8$ N/kg).

$$W = 60 \times 9.8 = 588 \text{ N}$$

## Momentum (Higher tier)

$$p = mv$$

(momentum = mass × velocity). Momentum is **conserved** in a closed system (e.g. a collision) — the total momentum before equals the total momentum after.

> **Exam tip:** All the equations on this page are given on the exam's equation sheet — you don't need to memorise them, but you do need to recognise which equation fits which situation, and be confident rearranging them.

## Required practical: investigating acceleration

Investigate how **force** affects acceleration (keeping mass constant), and how **mass** affects acceleration (keeping force constant) — typically using a trolley on a ramp/runway, a pulley system with weights providing the force, and light gates to measure velocity at two points (so acceleration can be calculated).

- **Independent variable**: force (or mass), depending on which part of the investigation.
- **Dependent variable**: acceleration.
- **Control variable**: mass (or force) — whichever isn't being tested.

The expected result: acceleration is **directly proportional** to force (for constant mass), and **inversely proportional** to mass (for constant force) — consistent with $F=ma$ rearranged as $a = F/m$.
