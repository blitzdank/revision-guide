## Energy stores and transfers

Energy is always **conserved** (never created or destroyed) — it's **transferred** between stores, or **dissipated** (usually as heat) to the surroundings, which generally becomes less useful.

Main energy stores: kinetic, thermal, chemical, gravitational potential, elastic potential, electrostatic, magnetic, nuclear.

Ways energy is transferred: mechanically (a force doing work), electrically, by heating, or by radiation (e.g. light, sound).

## Specific heat capacity

$$\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$$

where $\Delta E$ = change in thermal energy (J), $m$ = mass (kg), $c$ = specific heat capacity (J/kg°C), $\Delta\theta$ = change in temperature (°C). **This equation is given in the exam.**

Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C — different materials have different values, which is why some things heat up (or cool down) faster than others for the same energy input.

**Example:** find the energy needed to heat 2 kg of water (specific heat capacity 4200 J/kg°C) from 20°C to 80°C.

$$\Delta\theta = 80 - 20 = 60°C$$
$$\Delta E = 2 \times 4200 \times 60 = 504{,}000 \text{ J} = 504 \text{ kJ}$$

> **Exam tip:** Always work out $\Delta\theta$ (the *change* in temperature) first as its own step — plugging in a single temperature instead of the change is one of the most common mistakes with this equation.

### Required practical: determining specific heat capacity

Typical method: use an electrical heater to supply a known amount of energy (measured using $E = P \times t$, power × time) to a known mass of material, and measure the resulting temperature change. Rearranging $\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta$ for $c$ gives the specific heat capacity.

**Sources of error to know:** heat loss to the surroundings (not all the electrical energy goes into heating the material) means the calculated specific heat capacity is usually **higher** than the true value, since the equation assumes all the energy went into the material.

## Efficiency

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful output energy transfer}}{\text{total input energy transfer}}$$

(Or the same ratio using power instead of energy.) Often given as a percentage — multiply the fraction by 100.

**Example:** a motor is supplied with 500 J of energy and usefully transfers 350 J as kinetic energy. Find its efficiency.

$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{350}{500} = 0.7 = 70\%$$

No real machine is 100% efficient — some energy is always dissipated, usually by heating due to friction (in a mechanical system) or resistance (in an electrical one).

> **Exam tip:** Efficiency can never be more than 1 (or 100%) — if your calculated answer is bigger than that, you've made an error (often dividing the wrong way round).
