## The exam question

Paper 1, Section A gives you a **printed extract** from Macbeth and asks you
to write about it, **and then** to write about the same idea/theme/character
across the **whole play** (34 marks: 30 for the essay, 4 for AO4 spelling/
punctuation/grammar — this is the only Literature question marked for
accuracy). You don't choose the topic; it's set by the question, so you need
to know the whole play, not just a few favourite scenes.

## Plot in brief

Macbeth and Banquo, generals in the Scottish army, meet three witches who
prophesy Macbeth will become Thane of Cawdor, then king — and that Banquo's
descendants will be kings, though Banquo himself won't be. The first
prophecy comes true almost immediately. Urged on by his own ambition and by
Lady Macbeth, Macbeth murders King Duncan and takes the throne. Guilt and
paranoia follow: he has Banquo murdered (Banquo's ghost haunts him), then
Macduff's family. Lady Macbeth's guilt drives her to sleepwalking and
suicide. Macduff, whose family Macbeth slaughtered, kills Macbeth in the
final battle; Duncan's son Malcolm becomes king.

## Key themes

- **Ambition** — the play's engine. Macbeth's "vaulting ambition" leads him
  to murder; it's presented as a corrupting force rather than something
  admirable.
- **Guilt and conscience** — shown physically (blood imagery, Lady
  Macbeth's sleepwalking, hallucinations) rather than just stated.
- **The supernatural** — the witches blur fate and free will: do they
  predict Macbeth's future, or plant the idea that causes it?
- **Power and kingship** — Duncan and Malcolm represent a "rightful,"
  divinely sanctioned order; Macbeth's rule is shown as unnatural (disorder
  in nature follows his crimes).
- **Appearance vs reality** — "Fair is foul, and foul is fair"; Macbeth
  learns to "look like th' innocent flower, but be the serpent under't."
- **Masculinity** — Lady Macbeth repeatedly questions Macbeth's manhood to
  push him toward murder; the play interrogates what "being a man" means.

## Key quotations (public domain — quote these directly)

- *"Fair is foul, and foul is fair"* (Witches, Act 1) — introduces the
  play's confusion of appearance and reality.
- *"Stars, hide your fires; / Let not light see my black and deep
  desires"* (Macbeth, Act 1) — ambition he wants hidden even from himself.
- *"Screw your courage to the sticking-place"* (Lady Macbeth, Act 1) —
  her manipulation of Macbeth's resolve.
- *"Is this a dagger which I see before me"* (Macbeth, Act 2) — guilt and
  hallucination beginning before the murder is even committed.
- *"Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my
  hand?"* (Macbeth, Act 2) — guilt as something that can't be washed away.
- *"Out, damned spot!"* (Lady Macbeth, Act 5) — her guilt resurfacing in
  the sleepwalking scene, reversing her earlier confidence.
- *"Life's but a walking shadow... / Signifying nothing"* (Macbeth, Act 5)
  — his final nihilism once ambition has cost him everything.

## Context worth using

Macbeth was written around 1606, likely performed for King James I, who
believed in witchcraft and claimed descent from Banquo. The play's concern
with the **Divine Right of Kings** (the idea that a monarch's authority
comes from God, so killing a king is a crime against the natural order) and
witchcraft reflects Jacobean beliefs — using context accurately (not as a
history lesson, but to explain why something would have mattered to the
original audience) is part of AO3.

> **Exam tip:** Always answer on both the extract *and* the whole play —
> missing the whole-play half loses access to a large chunk of the marks,
> however good your extract analysis is.

> **Exam tip:** Use context to explain a choice, not as a separate
> paragraph bolted on. "Audiences in 1606 would have found the witches'
> prophecy genuinely unsettling, given James I's own well-known belief in
> witchcraft" is better than a standalone fact about Jacobean England.
