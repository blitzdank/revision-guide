## The compound growth/decay formula

**Given on the AQA formula sheet:**

$$\text{Final amount} = \text{Initial amount} \times (\text{multiplier})^n$$

where $n$ is the number of time periods (usually years), and the multiplier depends on whether it's growth or decay:

- **Growth** (increase): multiplier $= 1 + \dfrac{r}{100}$
- **Decay** (decrease): multiplier $= 1 - \dfrac{r}{100}$

where $r$ is the percentage rate per period.

## Worked example: compound interest (growth)

£2000 invested at 3% per year for 5 years. Multiplier $= 1 + \dfrac{3}{100} = 1.03$.

$$\text{Final amount} = 2000 \times 1.03^5 = 2000 \times 1.159274... = £2318.55 \text{ (to the nearest penny)}$$

> **Exam tip:** Keep the multiplier to its full decimal value through the calculation (don't round it early) — only round your *final* answer, to avoid losing accuracy.

## Worked example: depreciation (decay)

A car worth £15,000 loses 12% of its value each year. Multiplier $= 1 - \dfrac{12}{100} = 0.88$.

Value after 4 years:

$$15000 \times 0.88^4 = 15000 \times 0.599695... = £8995.43 \text{ (to the nearest penny)}$$

## Finding the rate or number of years

Sometimes you're given the start and end amounts and need to find $r$ or $n$ instead.

**Example:** a population of 500 grows to 648 over 2 years at a constant yearly rate. Find the rate.

$$648 = 500 \times m^2 \implies m^2 = 1.296 \implies m = \sqrt{1.296} = 1.1384...$$

So the multiplier is about 1.1384, meaning a growth rate of about **13.8% per year** (since $m = 1 + \frac{r}{100}$).

> **Exam tip:** If the question says "to the nearest percent" or similar, don't round the multiplier until the very last step — round only the final percentage.

## Reverse percentage (working backwards)

If you're told the *final* amount after a known growth/decay, divide instead of multiply.

**Example:** after a 15% sale discount, a jacket costs £51. What was the original price?

$$\text{Multiplier} = 1 - \frac{15}{100} = 0.85 \implies \text{Original price} = \frac{51}{0.85} = £60$$

> **Exam tip:** A common mistake is adding 15% back onto £51 instead of dividing — that gives the wrong answer, because 15% of the *original* price isn't the same as 15% of £51.
