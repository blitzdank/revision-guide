## Laws of indices

These apply whenever the base ($a$) is the same:

- $a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}$
- $a^m \div a^n = a^{m-n}$
- $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$
- $a^0 = 1$ (for any non-zero $a$)
- $a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}$ — a negative power means "reciprocal of the positive power"
- $a^{\frac{1}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{a}$ — a fractional power with a 1 on top is a root
- $a^{\frac{m}{n}} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m$ — root first, then power (usually easier to calculate)

> **Exam tip:** For $a^{\frac{m}{n}}$, always take the root *before* raising to the power when you can — the numbers stay much smaller. $8^{\frac{2}{3}} = (\sqrt[3]{8})^2 = 2^2 = 4$ is far easier than cubing 8 first.

## Surds

A **surd** is a root that can't be simplified to a whole number, like $\sqrt{2}$ or $\sqrt{5}$ — leaving an answer in surd form keeps it *exact*, unlike a rounded decimal.

**Simplifying:** look for a square factor.
$$\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \times 2} = \sqrt{25} \times \sqrt{2} = 5\sqrt{2}$$

**Multiplying surds:** $\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$.
$$\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{12} = \sqrt{36} = 6$$

**Expanding brackets with surds** works like normal algebra:
$$(\sqrt{2} + 1)(\sqrt{2} - 1) = 2 - \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{2} - 1 = 1$$

This particular pattern — $(\sqrt{a}+b)(\sqrt{a}-b)$ — always removes the surd, which is exactly how **rationalising the denominator** works.

## Rationalising the denominator

Exam answers should never be left with a surd on the bottom of a fraction.

**Simple case** — multiply top and bottom by the surd:
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$$

**Binomial denominator** — multiply top and bottom by the denominator with its sign flipped:
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}+1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}+1} \times \frac{\sqrt{2}-1}{\sqrt{2}-1} = \frac{\sqrt{2}-1}{2-1} = \sqrt{2}-1$$

> **Exam tip:** "Give your answer in the form $a + b\sqrt{c}$" is a common instruction — it tells you the final answer should still contain a surd, so don't try to round it to a decimal.
